Why Auto Loan Fraud Just Hit $10.4 Billion

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The call is coming from inside the house. For years, auto lenders built their defenses around the idea that fraud meant a criminal stealing some innocent person's identity to buy a car. That threat has not vanished, but it has become a sideshow. The real money, and the real growth, now comes from the person sitting across the desk at the dealership, filling out a loan application in their own name and lying through their teeth.


Auto lending fraud exposure in the U.S. has reached an estimated $10.4 billion, a figure that comes from Point Predictive's 2026 Auto Lending Fraud Trends Report. That is up 13 percent from the prior year's $9.2 billion, roughly double the 2015 figure. Stretch the timeline back further and the exposure has risen almost five-fold since 2010. The report analyzed millions of applications to map out exactly how the money is bleeding out, and the picture it paints is of a problem getting worse, not better.


The most important shift in auto finance fraud is who is doing it. First-party fraud — where an individual or a fraud ring misstates income, employment, or intent to repay, using a real or synthetic identity — now makes up close to 70 percent of all vehicle finance fraud. Put another way, 69% of the problem traces back to the real person applying, not some third-party identity thief working in the shadows.


This matters because lenders have spent most of their energy looking the wrong direction. They historically focused on stopping third-party attacks rather than first-party fraud, which is a bit like installing a deadbolt while leaving the windows open. Frank McKenna, chief innovation officer at Point Predictive, stated that applicants using real names to inflate earnings, misstate employment, launder credit, or spin up new profiles with CPNs drive the lion's share of fraud risk. McKenna also noted that these misrepresentation patterns often go undetected, which is precisely why they keep working.


The report splits the total exposure into six primary categories, and the breakdown tells you where the pressure is. Income and employment misrepresentation is the largest by a wide margin, accounting for 45.2 percent of total exposure — $4.68 billion. This is the oldest trick in the book dressed up for the digital age: a borrower pads a salary figure or fakes employment records to clear a loan amount they could never actually carry.

Synthetic identity fraud and credit washing together make up 24 percent of the total, or $2.52 billion. True name identity theft — the classic stolen-Social-Security-number scheme — contributes 15.4%, causing $1.60 billion in losses. Powerbooking and collateral fraud accounts for 7.2 percent, reaching $750 million. Bust-out fraud sits at 2.4 percent, or $250 million. Straw purchases round things out at 5.8 percent, a $600 million burden.


Synthetic identity fraud stitches genuine details together with invented ones to assemble a brand-new, bogus credit profile. It is dangerous because the losses run deep. Probable synthetic identities, once flagged, go on to default 12.5 times more often than typical consumers, and the typical case costs somewhere between $22,000 and $50,000. Those numbers dwarf what credit cards or personal loans usually lose to fraud.


Credit washing is a related trick where a consumer games dispute processes to wipe legitimate negative marks off a credit history. Criminals submit bogus FTC dispute affidavits along with fake police reports alleging identity theft, scrubbing bad trade lines before securing fresh auto financing. This one is growing fast — credit washing surged 228 percent between early 2024 and mid-2025.


Powerbooking is where a bad actor, sometimes a crooked dealer, tacks phantom features or options onto a car's listing. The lender then finances more than the vehicle is worth. A straw purchase happens when someone with good credit finances a vehicle for a buyer who would be turned down — and that hidden driver, usually with poor credit, eventually defaults.

Bust out fraud is the organized-crime end of the spectrum: rings establish multiple credit lines at once, grab several vehicles, then deliberately default on everything before lenders catch the pattern. It has grown 83% over the past five years.


The reason all of this is accelerating is that the barriers to committing fraud have collapsed. A fraudster can churn out fake paystubs and file automated credit disputes with almost no effort. Some fabricate pay stubs by hand or lean on AI, then pair them with forged credit reports. Others deploy automatic bot attacks to run schemes at scale.


Point Predictive's review of criminal Telegram channels found that chatter about AI and deepfakes for fraud jumped 644 percent between 2023 and 2024. Those channels featured AI-generated counterfeit identification documents, deepfake clips built to slip past identity checks, and synthetic identity generators. The upshot is that a scheme that once required skill and nerve now requires a subscription and a few prompts.


Here is the paradox: as the physical theft of cars drops, the financial theft of cars climbs. The National Insurance Crime Bureau has sounded the alarm — finance fraud is climbing even as vehicle thefts keep falling. Vehicle thefts dropped 21 percent in the first half of 2026 compared to the same period a year earlier, with all 50 states declining by some percentage. Stealing the metal has gotten harder; stealing the loan has gotten easier.

Timothy R. Slater, the NICB's chief operating officer, described the shift bluntly: "Vehicle finance fraud has evolved from isolated credit misrepresentations into an organized, digital criminal industry that touches dealerships, lenders and insurers." Slater also argued that stopping these networks "requires shifting away from reactive, siloed defenses toward standardized and synchronized cross-industry reporting."


Fraud is not an abstract line item. Recent research links roughly 24 percent of all auto loan defaults back to fraud, which means about a quarter of the loans going bad were poisoned from the application stage. Point Predictive's Early Payment Default Risk Index has climbed 25 percent over the last 24 months. And 77 percent of dealerships reported more fraudulent activity over the last two years, so this is not a problem confined to a few bad markets.


For individual dealers who cut corners, the math is brutal. Systematic dealer risk — padding car values over and over and attaching bogus employment to borrowers — can raise a lender's default risk for those dealers fivefold.


The fixes are not mysterious, but they require admitting the threat model has changed. Modern fraud detection tools, data sharing across industries, and consortium-based intelligence can meaningfully cut exposure. The trick is treating the applicant's own claims — income, employment, repayment intent — with the same suspicion once reserved for stolen identities.

Validating income and employment at the point of sale closes off the single biggest category. Flagging synthetic profiles before the loan funds stops the cases that default at 12.5 times the normal rate. Watching for credit-washing affidavits catches the fastest-growing scheme on the board.


None of this stops a determined criminal entirely, but it narrows the open windows. With losses expected to rise even further, the lenders that keep looking only for the stranger at the door are the ones who will keep paying for the person at the desk.

The question was could the redesigned Montego do as well or better? We’ll address that question at the end of the video for now let’s take a look at the 2nd generation Mercury Montego.

Summary

The second-generation Mercury Montego debuted in September 1971 as a 1972 model, bringing a complete redesign that shifted the car toward traditional American luxury. Although based on the Ford Torino, the new Montego featured body-on-frame construction, updated styling, a more comfortable ride, and a broad range of engines and trim levels. Initially, it outsold the first-generation model, but sales declined significantly over the following years as fuel prices rose and similar Ford and Mercury vehicles competed for buyers.

1972: A Complete Redesign

Mercury replaced the original Montego with a substantially different vehicle, abandoning the unibody construction for a body-on-frame design intended to deliver a more substantial, big-car ride. The two-door models used a 114-inch wheelbase, while four-door hardtops and station wagons rode on a 118-inch wheelbase.

Exterior styling included a prominent egg-crate grille, quad headlights, slab-sided bodywork, recessed door handles, wider C-pillars, and a large rear bumper incorporating the brake lights, turn signals, and backup lamps.

Five principal model offerings were available:

    • Montego: The base model.
    • Montego MX: Added exterior trim and upgraded sound insulation.
    • Montego MX Brougham: Featured deluxe wheel covers and a bench seat with a folding armrest.
    • Montego GT: A sportier model offered as a two-door fastback or wagon, with performance styling, bucket-seat options, and additional instrumentation.
    • Montego wagons: Available in MX and upscale MX Villager configurations, with the Villager receiving woodgrain exterior trim and upgraded interior appointments.

The engine lineup was extensive, ranging from a 250-cubic-inch inline-six producing 95 horsepower to several V8s. The most notable performance options included a 351 Cobra Jet V8 rated at 262 horsepower and a 429 Thunderjet V8 producing 205 horsepower. Transmission choices included a three-speed manual, an optional four-speed manual with a Hurst shifter, and a Select-Shift automatic.

The Cyclone name also returned, but only as an option package for the Montego MX and GT. It included distinctive decals, rocker-panel stripes, and restricted engine choices of the 351 Cobra Jet or 429 Thunderjet. With only 29 Cyclone-option cars built for 1972, it became one of Mercury's rarest performance offerings.

The automotive press responded favorably, with Road Test magazine naming the Montego MX Brougham its Car of the Year and the GT its Car of the Year for enthusiasts. Mercury sold 135,092 Montegos for 1972, exceeding the first-generation model's typical annual sales of approximately 100,000.

1973: Safety Regulations and Continued Growth

Federal regulations requiring passenger cars to withstand low-speed impacts without damaging safety or lighting components prompted Mercury to revise the Montego's front bumper, grille, and front fascia.

Mercury also reduced compression ratios across most of the engine lineup, lowering horsepower ratings. The 351 Cobra Jet retained its higher compression ratio and avoided the same change.

Despite these adjustments, sales climbed to 156,805 units, making 1973 the Montego's strongest year of this generation.

1974: The Shift Toward Luxury

The 1974 model year brought more substantial changes. The Montego platform was shared with the Mercury Cougar and Ford Elite, increasing the overlap among Ford Motor Company's midsize and personal-luxury offerings.

Federal requirements for rear bumpers capable of withstanding five-mph impacts led to another styling revision. Mercury discontinued the GT fastback, replacing its sporty appearance with an optional Sports Appearance Group featuring simulated hood scoops, racing mirrors, body stripes, raised-white-letter tires, a leather-wrapped steering wheel, and full instrumentation.

The engine lineup was revised considerably. The inline-six, 351 Cobra Jet, and 429 Thunderjet were discontinued. A new 460-cubic-inch V8 producing 220 horsepower became available, while the replacement 351 four-barrel V8 delivered just 163 horsepower.

Sales dropped to 91,458 units for 1974, a substantial decline from the previous year.

1975–1976: The End of the Line

By 1975, Mercury had repositioned the Montego as a more fully luxury-oriented car. The Sports Appearance Group disappeared, and the engine lineup became more limited. The standard V8 was a 351M (Modified) with a two-barrel carburetor producing 148 horsepower. Buyers could also choose 400- or 460-cubic-inch V8 engines.

Sales fell to 55,835 units in 1975. The 1976 Montego received no significant changes, and sales dropped further to 33,739 vehicles.

The 1976 model year marked the end of the Montego nameplate's second-generation run. The name would not return to a Mercury passenger car until 2005.

Why the Montego Ultimately Declined

The second-generation Montego initially succeeded because buyers appreciated its comfortable ride, traditional styling, and body-on-frame construction. However, several factors contributed to its eventual decline.

    • The 1973 fuel crisis: The Montego was relatively heavy and delivered unimpressive fuel economy just as gasoline prices and fuel-economy concerns became increasingly important.
    • Product overlap: The Mercury Cougar and Ford Elite shared its platform, while other Ford and Mercury models offered similar styling and features.
    • Changing market positioning: The Montego gradually moved away from its sportier roots toward luxury, losing some of its original identity.

By 1977, Mercury discontinued the Montego as a separate model. The Cougar expanded to include four-door and wagon versions, with the wagon retaining much of the Montego's bodywork behind the front doors and receiving a Cougar-style front end.

Overall Takeaway

The 1972–1976 Mercury Montego demonstrates both the appeal and the limitations of Detroit's midsize cars during the 1970s. Its redesigned chassis, comfortable ride, distinctive styling, and powerful V8 options made it a sales success at launch. The 1973 peak of 156,805 units showed that Mercury's new direction initially resonated with buyers.

However, rising fuel costs, reduced engine output, declining demand for large cars, and increasing similarities among Ford Motor Company's products undermined its long-term prospects. The Montego began as a successful reinvention of Mercury's midsize offering but gradually lost its market position, disappearing after 1976 as the company consolidated its lineup around the Cougar.


Porsche Rules Out U.S. Plant Even as Audi Bets $4 Billion

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Audi decided it wants a $4 billion plant in the United States. Porsche, which shares a corporate parent, a platform or two, and even a factory with its sister brand, wants no part of it.


"As things stand, should an Audi plant be built in the U.S., we do not plan to relocate Porsche production there," financial chief Jochen Breckner told analysts on an earnings call. The question was fair game, given that Audi and Porsche already build the Q7, Q8, and the related Cayenne under one roof in Slovakia.


Breckner was blunt about why. "The business case would not be advantageous for us, given the complexity of our product range, our lower production volumes and the additional investment that would be required," he said.


That is the honest version. Audi plays a volume game dressed up as a premium one. Porsche plays a margin game, where the real money lives in options, packages, pricier models, and the occasional extreme one-off. Its buyers are chasing performance and a badge, not a sticker price, which means the 15 percent tariff Trump slapped on imported cars last year stings less for Porsche than it would for a brand fighting over every dollar.


Then there is the capital question. Porsche has been struggling to post a meaningful profit, though it did manage to grow third-quarter earnings despite falling sales. A multibillion-dollar American factory is not in the budget, and by Porsche's own logic, not in the plan either.

Porsche's turnaround strategy, dubbed Sportwagenschmiede 35, leans hard into exclusivity. Building higher-volume models in Slovakia and Malaysia already chips at the "made in Germany" expectation baked into the brand. A U.S. line would chip harder.


There is one loose thread. A U.S. official said Audi's American plans would include the Q7 and Q8, which share a factory with the Cayenne. That technically cracks the door open for U.S.-built Porsches. For now, Porsche is keeping it shut.

The 2026 Honda CR-V adds a new TrailSport trim, giving the popular compact SUV a more rugged appearance while retaining the comfort, practicality, and everyday usability that make it a strong contender in the segment. Positioned around the middle of the CR-V lineup, the TrailSport comes exclusively with a hybrid powertrain and all-wheel drive.

The 2026 Honda CR-V TrailSport builds on the model's established strengths: comfort, practicality, everyday drivability, and a useful selection of technology features. Its hybrid powertrain and standard all-wheel drive add appeal, while exclusive styling elements give it a more rugged personality.

Its biggest limitation is that the TrailSport's off-road enhancements are largely cosmetic. It does not offer additional ground clearance or substantial upgrades for serious trail driving, and its backup camera and relatively small touchscreen trail some competitors.

Ultimately, the CR-V TrailSport is best suited to buyers who want a comfortable, efficient compact SUV with adventurous styling and all-weather capability, rather than a dedicated off-road vehicle.


Mazda Kills the CX-70 After Two Years, Folds It Into CX-90

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The CX-70 is finally confirmed by Mazda to be done. It lasted exactly two model years in the U.S. before Mazda pulled the plug. For 2027, it's gone, its two-row mission absorbed into a refreshed CX-90 that picks up new paint, added tech, and a suspension tune meant to quiet down the complaints.


The CX-70's problem was never a secret. It was a two-row CX-90 with the rear seats removed, down to keeping the CX-90's third-row cupholders, and it cost more than it should have. Buyers noticed. The CX-70 fell short of 14,000 deliveries in 2025, trailing every Mazda nameplate except the MX-5 Miata.


The 2026 numbers were worse. Through September, the CX-70 managed just 8,390 units, down 27.7 percent from the same stretch in 2025. The CX-90 isn't exactly thriving either, with 30,367 units over the same period and a 25.9 percent drop of its own. For context, Mazda's smaller CX-5 and CX-50 together racked up more than 246,000 deliveries in 2025.


Critics piled on where sales left off. The plug-in hybrid powertrain drew complaints for running rough. Consumer Reports flagged below-average predicted reliability. Motor Trend's long-term testing surfaced day-to-day quirks and infotainment glitches.

Mazda addressed the CX-90's most common gripes: highway vibrations and a stiff, harsh ride. The 2027 model gets a redesigned rear suspension crossmember for better behavior on rough pavement, and PHEV versions get a revised electric drive motor with more insulation, meant to cut noise at low speed.


Four new paint colors arrive, Navy Blue Mica, Aero Grey Metallic, Melting Copper Metallic, and Zircon Sand Metallic, each adding $595. Select trims now add roof rails, Mazda Online Navigation, and Alexa Built-In as standard.


Powertrains carry over, all with an eight-speed automatic and standard all-wheel drive: the mild-hybrid 3.3-liter turbo inline-six (280 horsepower, 332 lb-ft), the PHEV 2.5-liter four with electric motor and 17.8-kWh battery (323 hp, 369 lb-ft, up to 27 miles of electric range), and the Turbo S tune (340 hp on premium, 319 hp on 87 octane).

The base 3.3 Turbo Select climbs $400 to $39,700. The two-row options are now the 3.3 Turbo Premium Sport ($46,880) and 3.3 Turbo S Premium Sport ($53,540). PHEV pricing holds, starting at $50,695. The three-row range tops out at $57,970 for the Turbo S Premium Plus. Destination is $1,645.


One casualty worth noting: there's no two-row PHEV in the 2027 CX-90. Want that configuration, you'll be hunting leftover CX-70 inventory. The CX-90 is built in Japan, leaving it exposed to a 15 percent U.S. tariff.

Ford's Escape Site Teases a 2029 EV Comeback

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

Dead nameplates don't usually leave the door cracked open. The Ford Escape, whose production wrapped at the Louisville Assembly plant last December, has done exactly that.


When Ford discontinues a model, it typically converts the official landing page into a farewell. The Edge, for instance, gets the blunt treatment: its site declares "the end of the road." The Escape got softer language.


"Ford Escape is tapping the brakes," reads the banner on the official Escape page. Below it: "The Ford Escape SUV may be taking a breather for now, but the rest of our dynamic vehicle lineup is always going strong. Discover the perfect fit for the next phase of your Ford journey."


That is not the vocabulary of a dead model. "Taking a breather" and "for now" are doing a lot of work for a crossover that is no longer in production.


Whatever the copywriters are hinting at, the current Escape is effectively sold out. New dealer gross stock sat at roughly 500 units at the end of September, and the very last Escape built found a buyer a few weeks before that. The small, affordable crossover slot in Ford's lineup is empty.

The teasing isn't idle. The Escape is returning as a compact all-electric crossover built on the new Universal Electric Vehicle (UEV) platform. The reborn Escape carries the internal designation U833, is sized similarly to the outgoing model, and is set to begin production at the Louisville plant in 2029.


That makes it the second UEV-platform vehicle, coming after the Ford Fathom, which is due to begin building at the same plant sometime in 2027.


So the website coyness lines up with the plan. Ford knows the Escape name is coming back, it just won't be back for a while, and it won't run on gasoline. For buyers who wanted a cheap gas crossover in the meantime, that 2029 date is a long breather indeed.
